# I Built My Portfolio in 6–7 Days. Here's the Honest Story.

Everyone talks about *what* they built. Nobody talks about the 11 PM debugging sessions, the design decisions they scrapped three times, or the moment they almost gave up and used a template.

This is that story.

---

## Why I Even Started

I'm a CS student with internships, certifications, a research paper, and projects — but no single place that showed all of it together. My resume listed the work. My GitHub had the code. But neither of them told *me*.

I wanted a portfolio that felt like a person made it. Not a Bootstrap template, not a theme someone else designed. Something cinematic. Something mine.

So I opened VS Code and started from scratch.

---

## The Stack I Chose

**Next.js 14 + TypeScript + Tailwind CSS + Framer Motion.**

I picked these because I already knew them well — which meant I could focus my energy on *design decisions* instead of fighting the tools. That turned out to be the right call, because the design decisions were hard enough on their own.

---

## Day 1–2: Direction Before Code

The first two days, I barely wrote any code.

I was figuring out what I wanted the thing to *feel* like. I looked at dozens of portfolios — took notes on what worked, what felt generic, what I never wanted to do. I knew I wanted structure. Something editorial. Clean but not cold.

That's where the numbered sections came from — `01 — about`, `02 — skills`, all the way through. It wasn't just aesthetic. It gave the whole site a rhythm, like chapters in a book. The reader always knows where they are.

The branding came from a simple question: *what do I want someone to remember?* The answer was `MF.dev` — short, sharp, mine.

By the end of Day 2, I had a design direction and a rough wireframe. Zero components built. And that was okay.

---

## Day 3–4: Building the Core

Now the real work began.

The hero section took an embarrassing amount of time. I wanted it to feel alive — the "Building Tomorrow." headline, the `Available for opportunities` badge, the scroll indicator. Each of those tiny details went through multiple versions before I landed on something that felt right.

Then came the skills section. I had so many things to list — HTML, CSS, JavaScript, React, Next.js, TypeScript, Tailwind, Python, TensorFlow, DeepFace, Scikit-learn, Git, MySQL, Streamlit... the challenge wasn't showing them, it was organizing them so they didn't look like a wall of buzzwords. I broke them into categories — Programming Languages, Web Technologies, AI/ML, Tools — and added progress bars for the core ones. Suddenly it breathed.

The projects section was where I had to make real choices. I had two main projects to show: the **Face Recognition Attendance System** from my internship at OneYes InfoTech, and the **CKM Buildbase Website** from my web dev internship. I wrote the descriptions thinking about one person: a recruiter who has thirty seconds and doesn't know what DeepFace is. Clear features. Real outcomes. No jargon for the sake of it.

---

## Day 5: The Content Marathon

Day 5 was all content, and nobody warns you how hard content is.

Writing about your own work is strange. You know every line of it — which somehow makes it harder to describe objectively. For each experience entry — Cisco AI Internship, OneYes InfoTech, CKM Buildbase, ACM Student Chapter — I had to think: *what did I actually do here, and why does it matter to someone who wasn't there?*

The certifications section alone took longer than I expected. Ten certifications, all with IDs, all needing to link to LinkedIn verification. I wanted it to look like credentials, not a checkbox list. Clicking any certificate opens LinkedIn — that small interaction detail took maybe twenty minutes to get right.

The achievements section came naturally once I laid out the numbers: **9.30 CGPA. 2+ internships. 10+ certifications. 1 research paper.** Seeing them together like that was actually the first moment I felt proud of what I'd been building — not the portfolio, but everything it represented.

---

## Day 6: Debugging and Deployment

Day 6 was supposed to be polish. It became a debugging marathon.

Responsive design across devices. A mysterious layout shift on mobile that took two hours to track down. Framer Motion animations that worked perfectly on desktop and looked jittery on slower connections. Build errors on Netlify that made no sense locally.

There's a specific kind of frustration that comes with deployment. Everything works. Then you push it live and something breaks that never broke before. You fix that. Something else breaks. You start wondering if the whole thing was a mistake.

It wasn't. But Day 6 tested that belief thoroughly.

---

## Day 7: Ship It

The last day was refinement and letting go.

I checked every link. Tested the contact form. Made sure the Resume PDF opened correctly. Verified that all the LinkedIn certification links actually worked. Checked the site on my phone, on different browsers, in dark mode.

And then I pushed it live at **misy-portfolio.netlify.app**.

It's a strange feeling, publishing something personal. The moment it goes live, you immediately see ten things you want to change. I resisted the urge. The site was good. *Good enough* isn't settling — it's knowing when to stop polishing and start sharing.

---

## What I'd Tell Someone Starting Their First Portfolio

**Start with direction, not code.** Two days of thinking saved me a week of rebuilding things I'd built wrong.

**Write for the person who doesn't know you.** Your resume audience is a recruiter with limited time. Your portfolio audience is the same. Every section should answer: *why does this matter?*

**The small details are the whole thing.** The numbered sections. The `Available for opportunities` badge. The certification verification links. Nobody will notice them consciously — but they'll feel the difference between a site that has them and one that doesn't.

**Ship before it's perfect.** A live portfolio with a "Coming Soon" blog section beats a perfect portfolio sitting on localhost.


Six to seven days. A lot of iteration. One portfolio that finally feels like me.
